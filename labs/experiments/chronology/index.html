chronology tightness

What this is

Real dendrochronologists don't date a tree from one core sample — they build a master chronology: a composite ring pattern averaged from many trees of the same species and site, then cross-date individual specimens against it to see how well each one tracks the reference. This page builds the same thing for markets. For each asset class (crypto, equity, bonds, commodities, fx), every calendar year with at least 8 "alive" members (assets with a computed ring that year) gets a synthetic master ring: the median log-return and median realized vol across all members that year. Five master trees, one per class, each built from real per-asset annual stats but tracing no real asset's actual price path.

How to read it

The five large glyphs are the master trees — rendered with the exact same ring encoding as the seed forest (width = that year's growth, color = return, darkness = vol), except every ring is a median across the class, not one asset's real year. They carry no scars: a scar marks a real asset's real drawdown episode, and a median composite has no real price path to draw down from.

Under each master tree is an agreement heat strip, one cell per year: light means the class moved together that year, dark means it was a genuinely split year. Two numbers drive it — hover any cell for both: sign-agreement is the fraction of members whose direction (up/down) matched the master's; dispersion (MAD) is the median absolute deviation of member log-returns around the master, a stricter score that catches years where everyone agreed on direction but scattered wildly on magnitude. Cells with a colored tick underneath broke that class's own normal pattern (more than 1.2 standard deviations off its own baseline) — red for a split-sign year, amber for an unusually wide dispersion year.

The chronology tightness banner at the top ranks classes by their mean sign-agreement across all years — higher means that class's members more consistently move the same direction together. Each panel also names its single most divergent asset-year: the one (asset, year) pair with the largest gap between that asset's log-return and its class master's, that year. Click it (or search below) to open compare mode: the real asset's own tree next to its class master, plus a per-year deviation strip — green where the asset beat its master that year, red where it lagged, intensity scaled to the size of the gap.

The physics analogy — cross-dating

Dendrochronologists overlay many trees from a stand and read off the pattern they all share — that composite is the master chronology, and it's how a single sample tree (even a dead beam from an old building) gets dated: by matching its ring-width pattern against the reference. A tree that tracks the master tightly is easy to cross-date; one with erratic, idiosyncratic rings is not. Here, an asset that always agrees in sign and magnitude with its class master is "well cross-dated" against its class — a bond ETF that falls exactly when TLT falls, by about as much. An asset that regularly diverges — like a single-country FX pair breaking from the broader dollar move — is telling its own story, not the class's.

Caveats

The master is a statistical composite, not a tradable asset — nothing in this dataset actually earned the master's return. The universe is whatever data/rings.json currently tracks (a survivor-ish snapshot, not a point-in-time historical membership list), so early years have fewer members and the 8-member gate means the earliest 1-2 years of a class's real history may be excluded entirely if too few assets existed yet. Sign-agreement treats an exact zero return as agreeing with whatever sign the median happened to take — rare, but a real edge case. The current year (2026) is a partial year for every class, computed only over the months elapsed so far — its ring and agreement score will still move before December. Finally, "broke agreement" is relative to each class's own normal scatter, not an absolute bar — a genuinely diversified class like crypto has a much noisier baseline than bonds, so the same raw agreement number means something different in each.
