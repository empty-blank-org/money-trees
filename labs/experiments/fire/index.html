Fire Ecology

crashes as forest fires — serotiny scores assets that grow their widest rings right after a burn

What this is

Some conifers are serotinous: their cones are sealed shut with resin and only open to release seed when a fire's heat melts the seal. The species doesn't just survive fire — it needs it to regenerate. This page asks the same question of the money-trees universe: does a crash precede an asset's best growth, the way a burn precedes a serotinous pulse — or is a crash just a crash?

How to read it

Burn years reuse the exact scar gate from the Forest viz: a whole-history drawdown episode only counts as a "fire" if it's deeper than the asset's own median full-year realized volatility (clamped to 15–50%), and it has to stay underwater at least ~4 months (a fast V-recovery is a flesh wound, not a fire).

Serotiny score = mean(log return of the calendar year immediately following each burn year) − median(log return of all the asset's full calendar years). Positive means the asset's post-fire years beat its typical year; negative means fires are just followed by more of the same mediocrity. An asset needs at least 2 usable burn-then-recovery-year pairs to be scored at all — a single fire is an anecdote, not a pattern. The number shown next to each bar is n, the count of burns used.

The scatter plots that same score against each asset's annualized volatility, with the Pearson correlation printed on the chart and a fitted line — the direct test of whether "serotiny" is really just "volatile assets bounce hard," which is close to a truism for anything with fat-tailed annual returns.

The regrowth curves pick each asset's own local price trough inside four market-wide fires (COVID 2020, Terra/Luna 2022, the August 2024 yen-carry unwind, the 2025 tariff shock), then track median cumulative return from that trough forward, by asset class, at monthly resolution. A class-month is only drawn if at least 3 assets in that class actually have that much data past the trough — no simulated futures for events that haven't finished playing out (tariffs 2025 is still unfolding, so its lines simply stop early).

The analogy

Serotinous cones (lodgepole pine, some eucalypts) evolved for a habitat where periodic stand-replacing fire is normal: the parent trees die, but the fire also clears competing canopy and puts nutrients back in the soil, so the next generation's growth pulse is genuinely caused by, not just correlated with, the burn. The interesting question for a market asset is whether a drawdown plays the same causal role — clearing weak hands, resetting valuation, forcing a supply shock — or whether "biggest year right after the worst year" is just what mean reversion looks like in a volatile series.

Caveats

Survivorship. The data lake only contains assets that are still being tracked. Anything that burned and never regrew — delisted, rugged, wound to zero — isn't here to drag the average down. Every serotiny score on this page is upper-biased by construction; it can only measure the fires assets survived.

Mean-reversion confound. "The year after the worst year is unusually good" is close to definitionally true for any series with heavy-tailed annual returns, independent of any real ecological mechanism. The scatter panel is the honesty check for this — read its verdict line before trusting any single ticker's score.

Small-n scores. Most scored assets clear the ≥2-burn bar with exactly 2 or 3 burns. A score built on 2 data points is suggestive, not statistically solid — treat the ranking as a starting point for questions, not a conclusion.
